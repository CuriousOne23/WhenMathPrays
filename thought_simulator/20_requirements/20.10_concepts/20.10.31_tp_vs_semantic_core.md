## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the **conceptual foundation** for the relational identifier `20.10.31`, referred to in shorthand as `20.31`. It serves three interlocking functions:

1. **Definition** — It gives the phrase *semantic specification* a precise, bounded meaning within the relational numbering system, distinguishing it from adjacent concepts that share surface similarity.
2. **Structural Grounding** — It explains how the triple `20.10.31` encodes a directed, meaningful relationship between two conceptual nodes (`20` and `31`) through a specific predicate layer (`10`), and why that encoding matters for downstream consumers.
3. **Propagation Contract** — It makes explicit what any system, document, or process that *receives* or *emits* a reference to `20.31` is committing to — semantically, not merely syntactically.

This document is **not** an implementation guide. It does not specify data formats, schema fields, API contracts, or runtime behavior. Those concerns are handled by downstream specifications that reference `20.31` as an input dependency.

## 2. Anatomy of the Identifier: 20.10.31

The identifier `20.10.31` is a **relational triple** expressed in dot notation:

```
[Subject Node].[Predicate Layer].[Object Node]
      20      .       10       .      31
```

### 2.1 Subject Node: `20`

Node `20` designates the **specification domain** — the conceptual space concerned with defining, constraining, and communicating what things *mean* in a given system. This is the origin of the directed relationship. When `20` appears as a subject, the relationship it opens is always about *what is being specified* and *by what authority or frame*.

### 2.2 Predicate Layer: `10`

Layer `10` encodes the **semantic** predicate class — meaning-bearing, interpretive, intentional. This is the layer that distinguishes a *semantic* relation from a *syntactic* one (layer `01`), an *operational* one (layer `20`), or a *governance* one (layer `30`). Layer `10` asks: *what does this mean to a purposeful agent?* It implies that the relationship carries interpretive content — the connection is not merely formal but expresses intent, entailment, or conceptual dependency.

> **Key implication:** When layer `10` is the predicate, the relationship is *invalidated* if the meaning collapses — even if the structural form is preserved. A syntactically valid `20.10.31` expression that carries no interpretive content is a malformed instance, not a degenerate one.

### 2.3 Object Node: `31`

Node `31` designates **semantic specification** as a *target concept* — the thing being pointed to, defined, or constrained by the subject through the predicate. `31` is not a process; it is a *category of artifact or claim*. Specifically, it refers to the class of statements that:

- Carry meaning as their primary payload,
- Are directed at an interpreting agent (human or system),
- And are structured enough to be tested for coherence, completeness, or entailment.

### 2.4 The Shorthand `20.31`

The shorthand `20.31` **implies layer `10` as the canonical predicate** whenever no other layer is specified. This convention holds because layer `10` (semantic) is the only predicate layer whose content cannot be mechanically derived from the nodes alone — it requires interpretive commitment. Omitting the layer number is therefore a *strong* signal, not a lazy one: it asserts that the relationship is unambiguously semantic and that no disambiguation is needed.

**Do not use `20.31` when the predicate layer is contested or unclear.** Expand to the full triple in those cases.

## 3. What Semantic Specification Is

A **semantic specification** (`20.31`) is a structured claim about **meaning** — what a concept, entity, relation, or behavior *is*, *entails*, *excludes*, and *connects to* — expressed at a level of precision sufficient for a purposeful agent to act on it without further negotiation.

### 3.1 Core Properties

A valid `20.31` instance has all four of the following properties:

| Property | Description |
|---|---|
| **Intentionality** | The specification is *about* something — it has a referent that exists independently of the specification itself. |
| **Interpretability** | A purposeful agent (human or computational) can read the specification and derive consistent meaning without access to tacit or undocumented knowledge. |
| **Testability** | Claims made in the specification can, in principle, be assessed for coherence, consistency with other nodes, or entailment relationships. |
| **Boundedness** | The specification delimits what it covers and explicitly or implicitly acknowledges what falls outside its scope. |

All four must be present. A document that is intentional and testable but not interpretable without author context is a **working note**, not a semantic specification. A document that is intentional and interpretable but unbounded is a **conceptual survey**, not a semantic specification.

### 3.2 What `20.31` Produces

When applied, the `20.31` relationship produces one or more of the following artifacts:

- **Definition Statements** — Precise accounts of what a concept means and what falls under it.
- **Exclusion Statements** — Explicit descriptions of what the concept does *not* mean, especially where confusion is likely.
- **Relational Maps** — Statements of how this concept stands in relation to upstream, downstream, sibling, and orthogonal nodes.
- **Test Conditions** — Criteria by which an instance can be judged to satisfy or violate the specification.
- **Canonical Examples** — Concrete cases that anchor the abstract definition in graspable form.

### 3.3 The Interpretive Commitment

Declaring something a `20.31` artifact is a **commitment**, not a description. It obligates the author to:

1. Make meaning explicit rather than gestured at.
2. Maintain consistency with other nodes in the relational graph.
3. Propagate updates to downstream dependents when the meaning changes.
4. Distinguish personal intent from system-level meaning when the two diverge.

## 4. What Semantic Specification Is Not

### 4.1 Not a Syntactic Specification

A syntactic specification defines *form* — what a valid expression looks like, what characters are permitted, what structures are well-formed. It is concerned with parsability, not meaning. A grammar, a schema, an interface definition, and a type system are syntactic specifications.

`20.31` is agnostic about form. A semantic specification may be expressed in prose, structured tables, formal logic, or annotated diagrams. The *form* of the expression is irrelevant to its classification as `20.31`; what matters is whether it carries and communicates meaning.

> **Confusion pattern:** A schema with rich field descriptions reads like a semantic specification but remains syntactic. The descriptions explain the schema, not the world. Only when those descriptions make claims about meaning that hold *independently of the schema* does the document cross into `20.31` territory.

### 4.2 Not an Operational Specification

An operational specification describes *behavior* — what a process does, in what sequence, under what conditions, with what inputs and outputs. It answers "how does this work?" not "what does this mean?" Runbooks, workflow definitions, API behavioral contracts, and state machine specifications are operational.

`20.31` does not prescribe behavior. A semantic specification of the concept "authenticated session" does not tell a system how to authenticate users; it tells any system (or person) what must be true for a session to *count as* authenticated, and what it means when it does.

### 4.3 Not a Governance Document

Governance documents establish *authority and process* — who decides, by what mechanism, under what criteria, with what recourse. Policies, charters, decision frameworks, and approval workflows are governance artifacts.

`20.31` is not about who owns meaning; it is about what the meaning *is*. Governance documents may *cite* a semantic specification as the basis for a decision, but the specification itself does not contain governance logic.

### 4.4 Not a Conceptual Survey or Exploration

A conceptual survey maps a space of ideas without committing to a particular account of any of them. It is generative and open-ended. A semantic specification is selective and closed: it takes a position, draws a boundary, and commits to a definition.

Drafts, brainstorming notes, and "concepts" documents (such as this one in its early stages) are *precursors* to `20.31` artifacts, not instances of them. They graduate to `20.31` status only when the four properties in §3.1 are satisfied.

### 4.5 Not an Assertion of Truth

A semantic specification is a *claim within a relational system*, not a metaphysical truth claim. It says: "Within this system, using these frames, here is what this concept means and how it connects." The truth of the specification is relative to the coherence and integrity of the system — it can be wrong, outdated, or superseded, and the document should make its frame explicit.

## 6. Counterexamples

The following cases are frequently confused with `20.31` artifacts. Each fails to qualify for a specific, diagnosable reason.

---

### Counterexample 1: A Detailed README

A README file that explains what a software repository does, how to install it, and what its components are — even a thorough and well-structured one — is **not** a `20.31` artifact.

**Why it fails:**
- It is primarily *operational* (installation steps, usage commands) and *descriptive* (what the repo contains).
- It may be *intentional* (it is about the repo) but is not *testable* in the `20.31` sense — there is no claim whose truth-value is independent of the software's behavior.
- It describes what exists, not what things *mean*.

**What would make it qualify:** Adding a section that makes bounded, testable claims about the *conceptual purpose* of the system — not what it does, but what it *is* in the relational graph and what its existence entails for other nodes.

---

### Counterexample 2: A Glossary

A glossary of terms, even a precise and well-maintained one, is **not** a `20.31` artifact.

**Why it fails:**
- Glossaries define terms in isolation; `20.31` artifacts specify *relational meaning* — how a concept connects upstream, downstream, and laterally.
- Glossary entries typically lack **exclusion statements** (what the term does not mean) and **test conditions**.
- They are not **bounded** in the sense required: glossaries conventionally grow by accretion; a `20.31` artifact has a defined scope that delimits additions.

**What would make it qualify:** Structuring each entry as a full relational claim — including upstream dependencies, downstream entailments, and explicit exclusions — and committing the document to a bounded scope.

---

### Counterexample 3: A Philosophical Definition

"Meaning is that which is conveyed through intentional signs by a purposeful agent to an interpreting agent, in a context that renders the signs intelligible."

**Why it fails:**
- This is a *philosophical claim*, not a `20.31` artifact — it is not bounded to any relational system.
- It lacks a **referent within the system**: it does not specify what node or layer it is about.
- It is not **testable** in the system's terms — there is no mechanism by which an instance can be assessed against this definition within the graph.

**What would make it qualify:** Grounding the philosophical claim in a specific node (e.g., "Within the 20.x domain, node `31` adopts this account of meaning") and specifying what it entails for neighboring nodes in the graph.

---

### Counterexample 4: A Schema with Rich Annotations

A JSON Schema with `description` fields on every property is **not** a `20.31` artifact, even if the descriptions are detailed.

**Why it fails (the classic failure mode):**
- The descriptions *explain the schema's fields*, not the world. They are parasitic on the syntactic structure.
- Remove the schema, and the descriptions lose their referent.
- There are no **relational mappings** — the schema describes a data structure, not a position in a conceptual graph.

**What would make it qualify:** A companion document that specifies what the schema's concepts *mean* in the world — what real-world states of affairs would cause each field to be true or false — and how those meanings connect to upstream and downstream nodes.

---

### Counterexample 5: A Draft or Working Note

A document marked "WIP" that contains rich conceptual thinking but has not yet committed to bounded, testable claims is **not** a `20.31` artifact.

**Why it fails:**
- Drafts are *precursors*, not instances. They lack the commitment property described in §3.3.
- They may be intentional but are not yet interpretable without author context, and they are explicitly *not* bounded.

**Note:** This document is itself a counterexample until §9 (boundary conditions) is resolved and the four properties in §3.1 are verified to hold. A document can *describe* `20.31` without yet *being* one.

## 7. Upstream Mappings

Upstream nodes are those that `20.31` *depends on* — concepts or domains whose meaning must be stable before `20.31` can be coherently defined.

```
[10] Semantic Layer (Predicate Class)
  └─ defines the predicate used in 20.10.31
  └─ must be stable for the triple to carry valid meaning
  └─ dependency type: CONSTITUTIVE (not merely referential)

[20] Specification Domain (Subject Node)
  └─ provides the frame within which 31 is a target concept
  └─ determines the authority structure for meaning claims
  └─ dependency type: FRAMING

[00] Base Relational Grammar
  └─ defines the dot-notation syntax, node numbering conventions,
     predicate layer semantics, and shorthand rules
  └─ dependency type: FOUNDATIONAL (implicit in all triples)
```

### 7.1 Implications of Upstream Changes

| Upstream Node | Change | Effect on 20.31 |
|---|---|---|
| `[10]` Semantic Layer | Predicate layer `10` is redefined | All `20.10.31` triples must be revalidated |
| `[20]` Specification Domain | Scope of node `20` narrows or expands | Boundary of what counts as a specification changes; `20.31` may gain or shed instances |
| `[00]` Base Grammar | Dot notation or shorthand rules change | The shorthand `20.31` may become ambiguous; all consumers must be notified |

## 8. Downstream Mappings

Downstream nodes are those that *depend on* `20.31` — artifacts, processes, or concepts that take the meaning of `20.31` as an input and cannot be coherently specified without it.

```
[20.10.31] → [40.10.31] Implementation Specification
  └─ takes the semantic definition as input
  └─ specifies how systems satisfy or instantiate the meaning
  └─ dependency type: INTERPRETIVE (must preserve 20.31 meaning)

[20.10.31] → [20.30.31] Governance of Semantic Specification
  └─ establishes who owns, adjudicates, and updates 20.31 artifacts
  └─ dependency type: AUTHORITY (governance presupposes the thing governed)

[20.10.31] → [20.10.32] Adjacent Semantic Specifications
  └─ sibling nodes that must be distinguished from 31
  └─ dependency type: BOUNDARY (each sibling's meaning is partly defined
     by its distinction from 31)

[20.10.31] → [60.10.31] Evaluation and Validation Criteria
  └─ test conditions derived from 20.31's definition
  └─ dependency type: DERIVATION (test criteria cannot be correct if
     20.31's definition changes without re-derivation)
```

### 8.1 Propagation Obligations

When a `20.31` artifact changes in a meaning-relevant way (not merely editorial), the following propagation is required:

1. **Notify `[40.10.31]`** — Any implementation specification that references `20.31` must be reviewed for validity. Implementations that satisfied the old meaning may not satisfy the new one.
2. **Update `[60.10.31]`** — Validation criteria must be re-derived from the updated definition. Stale test conditions silently pass invalid instances.
3. **Reconcile siblings `[20.10.3x]`** — If the boundary between node `31` and its siblings shifts, sibling specifications must be updated to reflect the new boundary.
4. **Log the change in `[20.30.31]`** — Governance records must capture what changed, why, and when, so that consumers who cached the prior meaning can update.

## 9. Boundary Conditions and Edge Cases

### 9.1 When a Document Spans Multiple Node Types

A document may contain content that qualifies as `20.31` alongside content that does not (e.g., a specification that includes an implementation appendix). In this case:

- The document as a whole is **not** classified as `20.31`.
- The `20.31` sections may be extracted and referenced independently.
- The preferred pattern is modular authorship: separate documents for each node type, linked by explicit references.

### 9.2 When Meaning Is Contested

If two parties disagree about what a `20.31` artifact means, the artifact has failed its interpretability criterion — regardless of how clear it seemed to its author. Resolution procedure:

1. Identify the specific claim or term that is contested.
2. Trace to the upstream node (`[10]` or `[20]`) whose instability is producing the ambiguity.
3. Resolve at the upstream level first; then re-derive the `20.31` claim.
4. Do not patch the `20.31` artifact in isolation — this creates local clarity at the cost of systemic consistency.

### 9.3 The Draft-to-Specification Transition

A document transitions from draft to `20.31` status when:

- [ ] All four properties (§3.1) are verified to hold.
- [ ] Upstream dependencies (§7) are resolved and stable.
- [ ] At least one canonical example and one counterexample are provided.
- [ ] At least one downstream dependent has acknowledged the specification.

This document will reach `20.31` status when the above checklist is complete.

### 9.4 Retroactive Reclassification

If a document previously classified as `20.31` is found to violate one of the four properties, it must be retroactively reclassified (e.g., as a working note or conceptual survey) and all downstream dependents must be notified. Reclassification is not a failure — it is the system working correctly.

## 10. Governance and Versioning

### 10.1 Ownership

The `20.31` node is owned by the holder of the `20` (Specification Domain) authority. Changes to the node's meaning require that authority's sign-off. Changes to the predicate layer `10` semantics require the `[10]` authority's sign-off.

### 10.2 Versioning Conventions

- This document tracks the conceptual version of `20.31`, not the version of any artifact implementing it.
- Meaning-relevant changes increment the **major concept version** (e.g., `v1.0` → `v2.0`).
- Clarifications, examples, and editorial improvements increment the **minor concept version** (e.g., `v1.0` → `v1.1`).
- Version history is maintained in the governance record at `[20.30.31]`.

### 10.3 Review Triggers

This document should be reviewed and potentially updated when:

- Any upstream node (`[00]`, `[10]`, `[20]`) changes its definition.
- A new downstream dependent is registered.
- A counterexample is identified that the current definition does not correctly exclude.
- A real-world case arises that does not fit cleanly into §5 (examples) or §6 (counterexamples).

## Appendix A: Glossary

| Term | Definition in this document |
|---|---|
| **Semantic specification** | A structured claim about meaning that is intentional, interpretable, testable, and bounded. The class of artifacts at node `31`. |
| **Relational triple** | An expression of the form `[Subject].[Predicate Layer].[Object]` encoding a directed, meaningful relationship between two conceptual nodes. |
| **Predicate layer** | The middle segment of a relational triple. Encodes the *type* of relationship (semantic, syntactic, operational, governance, etc.). Layer `10` is the semantic layer. |
| **Subject node** | The origin of a directed relationship. In `20.10.31`, node `20` is the subject. |
| **Object node** | The target of a directed relationship. In `20.10.31`, node `31` is the object. |
| **Shorthand `20.31`** | The bare subject-object pair implying layer `10` as the canonical predicate. Valid only when the predicate is unambiguously semantic. |
| **Intentionality** | The property of being *about* a referent that exists independently of the specification. |
| **Testability** | The property of making claims that can, in principle, be assessed for coherence, consistency, or entailment. |
| **Interpretability** | The property of conveying consistent meaning to a purposeful agent without tacit or undocumented knowledge. |
| **Boundedness** | The property of having a defined scope that delimits what the specification covers and acknowledges what it does not. |
| **Propagation obligation** | The requirement to notify and update downstream dependents when an upstream specification changes in a meaning-relevant way. |
| **Constitutive dependency** | A dependency where the upstream concept is not merely referenced but is part of what makes the downstream concept what it is. |
